# 2 — Training and Adapting Models

> Pretraining buys knowledge, supervised fine-tuning buys format, preference optimization buys judgment, and LoRA makes the last two affordable. Know which stage a problem belongs to before you reach for fine-tuning.

---

## The pipeline

```
raw text ──pretrain──▶ base model ──SFT──▶ instruction model ──preference optimization──▶ assistant
 (trillions of tokens)   (continues text)   (follows the format)   (RLHF / DPO / RLAIF)    (helpful, harmless)
```

| Stage | Data | Objective | What it changes | Depth |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Pretraining | Web, books, code; trillions of tokens, unlabeled | Next-token cross-entropy | Knowledge, language, reasoning ability | This note; *LLMs from Scratch*, ch. 5 |
| Supervised fine-tuning (SFT) | Thousands to millions of (instruction, response) pairs | Same loss, on the response tokens | Format, instruction following, persona | This note; *LLMs from Scratch*, ch. 7; *LLM Engineer's Handbook*, ch. 5 |
| Preference optimization | Pairs of responses ranked by humans or a model | Reward model + PPO, or DPO directly | Which of several acceptable answers the model prefers | [05-RL](../../05-RL/README.md) |

The InstructGPT paper ([arXiv:2203.02155](https://arxiv.org/abs/2203.02155), Ouyang et al., 2022) is the reference recipe: demonstrations → SFT, comparisons → reward model, reward model → PPO. Its headline result: labelers preferred outputs of the 1.3B InstructGPT model to the 175B GPT-3. Alignment, not size, made the difference users noticed.

---

## Pretraining

Pretraining is self-supervised: the label for each position is the next token in the text. Raschka writes the loop by hand (*LLMs from Scratch*, ch. 5; full code at [rasbt/LLMs-from-scratch](https://github.com/rasbt/LLMs-from-scratch)):

```python
for input_ids, target_ids in train_loader:          # targets = inputs shifted one to the left
    logits = model(input_ids)                        # (batch, seq, vocab)
    loss = F.cross_entropy(logits.flatten(0, 1), target_ids.flatten())
    optimizer.zero_grad(); loss.backward(); optimizer.step()
```

One forward pass scores every position at once: the causal mask lets the model predict token 2 from token 1, token 3 from tokens 1–2, and so on, all in parallel. Feeding the true previous tokens rather than the model's own guesses is called *teacher forcing* (*Building AI Agents*, ch. 2). It is why training is parallel while generation (note 01, decode) is not.

Things worth knowing about the loss:

- **At initialization** the loss should be about `ln(vocab_size)`, the loss of a uniform guess: ≈ 10.8 for a 50,257-token vocabulary. Raschka's untrained GPT-2 scores 10.7940 on his first batch, a perplexity of about 48,725, close to the vocabulary size (*LLMs from Scratch*, ch. 5). If it starts far higher, the initialization is broken.
- **Perplexity** = `exp(loss)`: the effective number of tokens the model is choosing between.
- **Overfitting looks like this.** Raschka trains the 124M model for 10 epochs on one short story (Edith Wharton's "The Verdict", 5,145 tokens, 90/10 train/validation split, context cut to 256). It takes about five minutes on a MacBook Air. Training loss falls from 9.78 to 0.39 while validation loss stalls near 6.4, and the model starts reciting passages of the story verbatim. Real pretraining makes roughly one pass over a far larger corpus, with batch sizes of 1,024 or more. Verbatim recall survives that, though. GPT-2 XL (1.5B) had a training loss only about 10% below its test loss. Even so, Carlini et al. sampled 600,000 generations from it, ranked them with six membership-inference scores (the most precise compared the model's likelihood with zlib compression), and checked the top 1,800 candidates by hand. 604 were memorized training text, including names with phone numbers, 128-bit UUIDs and code. Some strings appeared in a single document of the training set ([arXiv:2012.07805](https://arxiv.org/abs/2012.07805) (Carlini et al., USENIX Security 2021)). The 604 are a lower bound from one model; the authors could not measure recall over the whole training set.

- **Save the optimizer state with the weights.** AdamW keeps two running statistics per parameter. Resume from weights alone and the first steps behave like a fresh optimizer (*LLMs from Scratch*, ch. 5). LLM360 made that mistake at scale: Amber's training code saved no optimizer state, so AdamW restarted from scratch at every resume. The authors suspect this caused the NaN losses that made them drop 4 of 360 data chunks ([arXiv:2312.06550](https://arxiv.org/abs/2312.06550) (Liu et al., 2023, preprint)). That state is most of the training memory. With mixed precision, Adam needs about 16 bytes per parameter: 2 for fp16 weights, 2 for fp16 gradients, and 12 for the fp32 master weights, momentum and variance. A 1.5B GPT-2 therefore needs at least 24 GB before activations, against 3 GB for its fp16 weights. ZeRO's answer is to shard that state across the data-parallel GPUs instead of copying it to each one. Sharding the optimizer state cuts per-GPU model-state memory 4×, and sharding the gradients as well cuts it 8×, at the same communication volume as plain data parallelism. With that, models of up to 13B trained with no model parallelism on 32 GB V100s, where plain data parallelism ran out of memory above 1.4B ([arXiv:1910.02054](https://arxiv.org/abs/1910.02054) (Rajbhandari et al., SC 2020)). Sharding the weights too (stage 3, the scheme PyTorch FSDP's full sharding follows) was described but not yet built at publication. The "W" in AdamW is *decoupled* weight decay. Under Adam, an L2 penalty is divided by the adaptive denominator, so weights with large gradients are barely regularized. Shrinking the weights directly, outside the gradient, cut test error by about 15% relative on CIFAR-10 and ImageNet 32×32 ResNets, and made the best learning rate independent of the best decay ([arXiv:1711.05101](https://arxiv.org/abs/1711.05101) (Loshchilov & Hutter, ICLR 2019)). That evidence is image classification only; the LLM default came by adoption.

Real pretraining runs add data curation (deduplication, quality filtering, mixing code and text), learning-rate warmup and cosine decay, gradient clipping and mixed precision. Clipping does not prevent every blow-up. PaLM 540B's loss spiked about 20 times during training, at irregular intervals and sometimes late, although none of the smaller models spiked. The fix was to restart about 100 steps before the spike and skip 200–500 batches. Replaying those same batches from an earlier checkpoint caused no spike, so the authors blame the combination of data and parameter state, not bad data. PaLM also dropped all bias terms and added an auxiliary *z-loss* (10⁻⁴ · log²Z) that keeps the softmax normalizer near 1, both for stability ([arXiv:2204.02311](https://arxiv.org/abs/2204.02311) (Chowdhery et al., JMLR 2023)). Raschka also loads the released GPT-2 weights into the hand-built model, which proves the architecture in [note 01](01-how-llms-work.md) matches the real one (*LLMs from Scratch*, ch. 5).

**Most "open" models release only the weights, which hides those steps.** LLM360 scored 16 open models, from GPT-J (2021) to Yi (2023), on seven artifacts: code, configs, weight checkpoints, optimizer checkpoints, data mix, data order and the data itself. Disclosure shrank as the models got stronger. Llama 2, Qwen, Mistral and Yi released weights and little else. LLM360's Amber (6.7B, 1.26T tokens) released all seven, with 360 checkpoints. It copied LLaMA's hyperparameters and still trailed LLaMA-7B, 45.2 to 49.7 averaged over four leaderboard tasks. The authors blame LLaMA's undisclosed data ([arXiv:2312.06550](https://arxiv.org/abs/2312.06550) (Liu et al., 2023, preprint)). OLMo released its 2.7T-token corpus (Dolma), more than 500 checkpoints, its training logs and a reproducible data order, under Apache 2.0 ([arXiv:2402.00838](https://arxiv.org/abs/2402.00838) (Groeneveld et al., ACL 2024)). OLMo 2 closed most of the gap. Its 7B model, trained on 4.05T tokens, averaged 62.9 on ten benchmarks against Llama 3.1 8B's 61.8, with about a quarter of the training compute (1.8 against 7.2 × 10²³ FLOPs). The last 5–10% of that compute is a *mid-training* stage: the learning rate decays to zero on a smaller mix of filtered web pages, academic text and synthetic math, and three or four such runs are averaged into the final model ([arXiv:2501.00656](https://arxiv.org/abs/2501.00656) (OLMo et al., 2024, preprint)). Open-weight frontier reports give that stage much more. GLM-4.5, a 355B mixture-of-experts model with 32B active, pretrained on 22T tokens: 15T of general text, then 7T with code, maths and science upweighted. Its highest-quality web bucket was repeated more than 3.2 times. It then mid-trained on about 1.1T more tokens. That was 500B of repository-level code with issues and pull requests, 500B of synthetic reasoning traces and 100B of long documents and agent trajectories, while the context grew from 4K to 32K and then 128K ([arXiv:2508.06471](https://arxiv.org/abs/2508.06471) (GLM-4.5 Team, 2025, preprint)). It used Muon, and kept cosine decay after a warmup-stable-decay schedule underfit in early tests. The report has no ablation of the mid-training stages. The FLOPs are 6ND estimates and the benchmarks are Ai2's own suite, though four of the ten were held out from development.

**Batch size and learning rate move together, up to a limit.** Goyal et al. trained ResNet-50 on ImageNet with a batch of 8,192 on 256 GPUs in one hour and matched the batch-256 top-1 error (23.74% vs 23.60%). They multiplied the learning rate by the batch-size ratio and ramped it up linearly over the first 5 epochs. Without the warmup, error was 24.84%; past 8k it rose anyway, to 27.55% at 32k ([arXiv:1706.02677](https://arxiv.org/abs/1706.02677) (Goyal et al., 2017, preprint)). This is where the linear warmup in LLM recipes comes from, although the evidence is SGD on images. McCandlish et al. explain where the limit sits. The *gradient noise scale*, the gradient's variance divided by its squared norm, predicted the critical batch size to within an order of magnitude on tasks ranging from about 20 (an SVHN autoencoder) to over 10 million (Dota 5v5). At that batch, training takes twice the minimum number of steps and twice the minimum data. The noise scale grows as the loss falls, often 10× over a run, so later training tolerates bigger batches ([arXiv:1812.06162](https://arxiv.org/abs/1812.06162) (McCandlish et al., 2018, preprint)). Their model rests on training loss and a local quadratic approximation.

**Newer optimizers go after AdamW's memory and its batch limit.** Adafactor stores only row and column averages of the squared gradient, O(n + m) memory for an n × m matrix instead of O(nm), and can drop momentum. On a WMT'14 En→De Transformer, the factored version without momentum matched Adam (BLEU 25.4 vs 25.4, with warmup). Without warmup, its β₂ = 0.999 setting collapsed (BLEU 0.2) unless updates were clipped (22.4) ([arXiv:1804.04235](https://arxiv.org/abs/1804.04235) (Shazeer & Stern, ICML 2018)). Muon keeps a single momentum buffer and orthogonalizes each matrix update with a Newton–Schulz iteration. Across models of 100M–4B parameters and batches up to 16M tokens, it reached AdamW's loss with 10–15% fewer tokens, and the gap held beyond the critical batch. Hyperparameters transferred with μP up to 3.7B ([arXiv:2505.02222](https://arxiv.org/abs/2505.02222) (Essential AI, 2025, preprint)). Both comparisons are on training loss. NVIDIA took Muon and SOAP to an 8B dense model, a 30B-A3B MoE and a 72B-A8B hybrid on 1–3T tokens. Both stayed stable up to batches of about 100M tokens, where AdamW degraded. Downstream gains were uneven: the 72B-A8B hybrid with Muon at twice the batch scored 4.5 points higher than AdamW on coding and 3.3 points lower on math. Plain SOAP diverged at 8B until they changed how it estimates its preconditioner. They also scale the learning rate by the square root of the batch ratio, not Goyal's linear rule, for large batch jumps ([arXiv:2607.20548](https://arxiv.org/abs/2607.20548) (Khona et al., 2026, preprint)).

Not every curation step is a guaranteed win. Pythia trained eight model sizes, from 70M to 12B parameters, twice each: once on the Pile (300B tokens) and once on a near-deduplicated Pile of 207B tokens, run for about 1.5 epochs so that it also reached 300B. Both runs used the same data order and saved 154 checkpoints ([arXiv:2304.01373](https://arxiv.org/abs/2304.01373) (Biderman et al., 2023-04)). On eight standard benchmarks, deduplication showed "no clear benefit", and seeing the data a second time did no visible harm. The same suite showed that memorized 32-token sequences turn up at a steady rate through training, fitting a Poisson process. So putting sensitive data early or late in training does not change how much of it gets memorized. Each size was trained with one seed; the authors estimate that 25 seeds would have cost about $10 million. The 12B model alone took 72,300 A100-hours.

Deduplication pays off on memorization more than on benchmarks. In C4, Lee et al. found one 61-word sentence repeated 61,036 times, and 4.6% of validation examples had a near-duplicate in training. They trained 1.5B models for about two epochs on the original and on deduplicated copies. On the original C4, 1.93% of tokens in unprompted generations sat inside a 50-token exact match with training data. On the deduplicated copies it was 0.14–0.19%, about 10× less, at equal or better perplexity ([doi:10.18653/v1/2022.acl-long.577](https://doi.org/10.18653/v1/2022.acl-long.577) (Lee et al., ACL 2022)). They ran no downstream tasks, so their result and Pythia's benchmark null measure different things. Goldfish loss tackles memorization in the objective. It leaves 1 in k tokens out of the loss, chosen by hashing the 13 preceding tokens, so every copy of a passage is masked in the same places. LLaMA-2-7B trained for 100 epochs on 100 Wikipedia articles reproduced 84 of them verbatim with the standard loss and none with k = 4. In a normal 1.1B run on 20B tokens, benchmark scores barely moved. The price is that it needs about a third more raw tokens (26.7B) to match a 20B-token standard run ([arXiv:2406.10209](https://arxiv.org/abs/2406.10209) (Hans et al., NeurIPS 2024)). It is not a privacy guarantee. In the 1.1B run, where 2,000 Wikipedia documents were repeated 50 times, a zlib-based membership-inference attack still flagged 88.5% of those documents at a 0.1% false-positive rate, against 99.75% with the standard loss. How much can a model hold at all? Morris et al. trained GPT-style models of up to 20M parameters on random bitstrings, which cannot be learned except by memorizing them, and measured about 3.6 bits per parameter. On deduplicated text, models memorized until that capacity was full, then began to generalize, with double descent at that point ([arXiv:2505.24832](https://arxiv.org/abs/2505.24832) (Morris et al., 2025-05, preprint)). For facts rather than random bits, Allen-Zhu & Li measured about 2 bits per parameter. They trained models from scratch on synthetic biographies, (name, attribute, value) facts written out as text, and the figure held across GPT-2, LLaMA and Mistral sizes once each fact had been seen 1,000 times. At 100 exposures it fell to 1 bit per parameter. Quantizing to int8 kept the full capacity and int4 cut it to 0.7. Mixing in junk data at 7 junk tokens per useful one cut capacity for the useful facts 20-fold. Prepending a domain-style tag to the useful data brought that loss down to 2× ([arXiv:2404.05405](https://arxiv.org/abs/2404.05405) (Allen-Zhu & Li, 2024-04, preprint)). Their fitted law says loss-based membership inference falls to chance at about 100 tokens per parameter, a ratio the authors say contemporary LLMs already exceed. They checked the law only on GPT-2 models up to 1.5B, and it covers a loss-threshold attack only, not extraction of rare strings like Carlini's UUIDs.

### What the data is

Most pretraining text starts as Common Crawl, a scrape of billions of web pages, usually filtered into something like Google's C4. Filtering is crude: GPT-2's WebText kept only pages linked from Reddit posts with at least three upvotes (*AI Engineering*, ch. 2). C4 itself was one month of Common Crawl (April 2019). T5's authors kept only lines that end in terminal punctuation and dropped pages with fewer than three sentences, a word from a bad-word list or a curly bracket. They deduplicated repeated three-sentence spans and kept pages that langdetect scored at least 0.99 English, which left about 750GB ([arXiv:1910.10683](https://arxiv.org/abs/1910.10683) (Raffel et al., JMLR 2020)). With their 220M baseline trained on 34B tokens, the 6.1TB unfiltered version scored worst on every task (GLUE 81.46 against 83.28). Small in-domain sets won on matching tasks: Wikipedia plus books raised exact match on the fiction-heavy MultiRC from 25.78 to 50.93. Repetition hurt only at extremes. Cycling a small subset 1,024 times cut GLUE to 79.55, while 64 repeats barely moved it. Books entered through BookCorpus, which was gathered for a different job: training sentence embeddings to align novels with their film adaptations. It holds 11,038 free books by unpublished authors, each over 20,000 words, about 985M words in all, and Romance is the largest genre at 2,865 books ([arXiv:1506.06724](https://arxiv.org/abs/1506.06724) (Zhu et al., ICCV 2015)). The paper says nothing about licensing, consent or duplicates, yet the corpus went on to train GPT-1 and BERT. The mix decides what the model is good at. English is 45.88% of Common Crawl and Russian, second, about 6%, which shows up later as worse quality and higher token cost in other languages (note 01, Tokens). mT5 rebuilt C4 for 101 languages from all 71 monthly Common Crawl scrapes, replacing the English punctuation rule with a line-length filter. It sampled each language in proportion to its size raised to the power 0.3, which boosts the low-resource ones ([doi:10.18653/v1/2021.naacl-main.41](https://doi.org/10.18653/v1/2021.naacl-main.41) (Xue et al., NAACL 2021)). On zero-shot XNLI with mT5-Large, raising the exponent to 0.7 helped Russian (81.5 to 82.8) and hurt Swahili (75.4 to 70.6), and dropping the line-length filter cost 2 points. Sharing capacity across 101 languages cost the small models: mT5-Small trailed English-only T5-Small on SQuAD (84.7 against 87.2 F1), and the gap had almost closed at XXL (95.6 against 96.2). Newer reports filter with a model instead. Qwen2.5 used Qwen2-Instruct as the quality filter, downsampled e-commerce, social media and entertainment, upsampled science and technology, and grew the corpus from 7T to 18T tokens ([arXiv:2412.15115](https://arxiv.org/abs/2412.15115) (Qwen Team, 2024-12)). An earlier Phi model, phi-1.5, leaned on synthetic data even harder. It carried phi-1's synthetic Python "textbooks" ([arXiv:2306.11644](https://arxiv.org/abs/2306.11644) (Gunasekar et al., 2023-06, preprint)) over to common-sense text. The 1.3B model trained on 150B tokens drawn from a 30B-token set. About 20B of that set was synthetic "textbook" text generated from 20K seed topics, and the only real data was 6B tokens of filtered code. It used about 1.5K A100-hours of training compute. It matched Llama2-7B on common sense (WinoGrande 0.734 vs 0.691) and trailed it on knowledge (MMLU 0.376 vs 0.453) ([arXiv:2309.05463](https://arxiv.org/abs/2309.05463) (Li et al., 2023-09, preprint)). The generation recipe is described only as "intricate iterations", and there is no contamination analysis. Phi-3 pushed filtering hardest, keeping heavily filtered web data plus synthetic text in what Microsoft calls a "data optimal regime" ([arXiv:2404.14219](https://arxiv.org/abs/2404.14219) (Abdin et al., 2024-04)). **phi-3-mini (3.8B, 3.3T tokens) scored 68.8% on MMLU 5-shot, close to Mixtral 8x7B's 70.5%, but 64.0 on TriviaQA against Mixtral's 82.2.** Filtering kept reasoning and gave up stored facts. Both results come from Microsoft's internal eval pipeline, and the data was not released. Filtering can also remove a capability on purpose. With medicine as a proxy forget domain, dropping or loss-masking only the medical *tokens* hit medicine as hard as dropping whole documents and cost less on neighbouring biology. **At 1.8B parameters, matching an unfiltered model's medical loss would take about 7000× the compute.** It held up 10× better than an unlearning baseline against adversarial fine-tuning, and the filtered models still learned to refuse medical questions ([arXiv:2601.21571](https://arxiv.org/abs/2601.21571) (Rathi & Radford, 2026-01, preprint)). The evidence is that single proxy domain, at up to 1.8B parameters.

The mixture weights can be learned rather than hand-set. DoReMi trains a 280M proxy model with Group DRO, which upweights whichever domain the proxy is furthest behind a reference model on, and reuses its domain weights for an 8B model. On the Pile, the weights moved Pile-CC from 11% to 61% of the mix and cut arXiv from 10.5% to 0.4%. The 8B model gained 6.5 points of average one-shot exact match on five generative QA tasks, reached the baseline's accuracy in 2.6× fewer steps, and improved perplexity on all 22 domains. The tuning cost 8% of the 8B run's compute ([arXiv:2305.10429](https://arxiv.org/abs/2305.10429) (Xie et al., NeurIPS 2023)). A 1B proxy did worse than the 280M one, and the authors cannot explain why the weights transfer across scales. DSIR selects documents instead of domains. It resamples raw data so its hashed n-gram distribution matches a target such as Wikipedia and books, and it processed 100M Pile documents in 4.5 hours on one CPU node. A BERT-base trained from scratch on the selection scored 82.30 on GLUE against 80.25 for random data ([arXiv:2302.03169](https://arxiv.org/abs/2302.03169) (Xie et al., NeurIPS 2023)). That is masked-LM evidence only, and n-grams are a surface signal. Code is in nearly every mix on the belief that it teaches reasoning. A survey of that belief cites GSM8K chain-of-thought scores rising from 12.4 for text-davinci-001 to 46.9 for code-trained text-davinci-002 ([arXiv:2401.00812](https://arxiv.org/abs/2401.00812) (Yang et al., 2024-01, preprint)). Those are different models, and the survey concedes that explicit causal evidence is still missing. Ruis et al. looked at the data side. They ranked 5M pretraining documents (2.5B tokens) of Cohere's Command R 7B and 35B by influence on 40 simple maths queries. Answers to factual questions turned up in the top 500 documents for 55% of queries at 7B. Answers to reasoning questions almost never did. The influential documents instead showed the procedure, often as code or formulas, and StackExchange appeared about 10× more often than its share of the data ([arXiv:2411.12580](https://arxiv.org/abs/2411.12580) (Ruis et al., ICLR 2025)). The sample is 2.5B tokens out of trillions, influence is computed only through MLP weights, and code was over-represented for control queries that need no reasoning. Capabilities also come from data nobody labelled. 1.4% of PaLM's training instances were bilingual, holding over 30M translation pairs across 44 languages. Removing the translation pairs, about 0.5% of examples, cost a 1B model 7.4 BLEU on zero-shot translation into English from high-resource languages, but only 2–3 BLEU at 8B ([arXiv:2305.10266](https://arxiv.org/abs/2305.10266) (Briakou et al., ACL 2023)). That evidence covers PaLM's data and English-paired translation only.

Code corpora show what a documented pipeline involves. BigCode's StarCoder started from The Stack v1.2, 6.4TB of permissively licensed GitHub code in 384 languages, with an opt-out form; 44 people had opted out when the data was processed ([arXiv:2305.06161](https://arxiv.org/abs/2305.06161) (Li et al., TMLR 2023)). It kept 86 languages, near-deduplicated with MinHash, and removed files matching HumanEval, MBPP, APPS, GSM8K or DS-1000. To redact personal data, 1,399 crowd workers labelled 22,950 entities in 12,000 files, which trained an encoder to find secrets. It detected keys at 70.4 F1, against 6.7 for a regex baseline. StarCoderBase (15.5B) trained on 1T tokens, and StarCoder added 35B Python tokens on top. The paper also shows HumanEval misleading. CodeGen-16B-Mono came close to StarCoderBase on HumanEval (29.3 vs 30.4 pass@1) but scored half as much on DS-1000's data-science tasks (11.7 vs 23.8). The authors concede the opt-out works per repository, so copies of the same code in other repositories stay in the data, and that the model can still emit personal data. OpenCoder released its whole pipeline and its 960B-token corpus with ablations at 1.5B ([arXiv:2411.04905](https://arxiv.org/abs/2411.04905) (Huang et al., 2024, preprint)). About 75% of GitHub files were exact duplicates. On 485M Python files, deduplicating per file kept 32.7B tokens and deduplicating per repository kept 99.5B, yet the file-level corpus trained the better model on HumanEval and MBPP. Keeping only repositories with at least 5 GitHub stars, as SantaCoder had also tested, lowered training loss but made the model worse, which the authors put down to lost diversity. Dropping the algorithmic and synthetic data from the final annealing stage also hurt. These comparisons are shown only as training curves, one run per setting.

Two limits are closing in, according to Huyen (*AI Engineering*, ch. 2). **Data:** models consume public text faster than people write it (Villalobos et al., 2022), and sites are shutting crawlers out; Longpre et al. (2024) found 45% of C4 restricted by terms of service or robots.txt after 2023–24. Training on model-generated text is one answer, but *Building AI Agents* (ch. 3) warns of *model collapse*: studies in which models trained on synthetic data lost performance or forgot skills they had learned. Rephrasing real pages is safer than generating new ones. WRAP had Mistral-7B rewrite C4 in Wikipedia and Q&A styles and trained on the rewrites mixed with the originals. A 1.3B model reached the same zero-shot accuracy about 3x faster, and Pile perplexity fell by about half. Training on the rewrites alone hurt perplexity on messy real text ([arXiv:2401.16380](https://arxiv.org/abs/2401.16380) (Maini et al., ACL 2024)). **Electricity:** data centres used an estimated 1–2% of world electricity at the time of writing, projected to reach 4–20% by 2030.

### What it costs

Count training compute in **FLOPs** (operations) and hardware speed in **FLOP/s** (operations per second); the two are easy to confuse (*AI Engineering*, ch. 2). GPT-3 175B took 3.14 × 10²³ FLOPs. Huyen's estimate for redoing it on 256 H100s at a peak of 60 TFLOP/s, with no failures, is about 236 days, and at 70% utilization and $2 per H100-hour, over $4 million. Two cautions. First, 60 TFLOP/s is roughly the H100's 32-bit rate; training runs in BF16 on tensor cores, whose dense peak is on the order of 1,000 TFLOP/s, so the real time and cost would be an order of magnitude lower. Second, the book's cost formula multiplies by 256 days, not the 236 in its own text. Read the example as a method, not a quote.

Raschka's estimate for an open model (*LLMs from Scratch*, ch. 5): Llama 2 7B took 184,320 A100 GPU-hours on 2 trillion tokens, about $690,000 at $30 an hour for an 8×A100 cloud server. He cites an estimate of about $4.6 million in cloud credits for GPT-3's pretraining (*LLMs from Scratch*, ch. 1). The 184,320 is from Meta's own table, which puts the whole Llama 2 family (7B, 13B, 34B and 70B, each on 2T tokens) at 3.3M A100-hours and 539 tCO2e. Those figures assume every GPU drew its rated peak power (350–400 W), and they leave out cooling, networking and the rest of the server ([arXiv:2307.09288](https://arxiv.org/abs/2307.09288) (Touvron et al., 2023, preprint)).

Growing the model during training is one way to cut the bill. FLM-101B trained a 16B model on 245B tokens, then expanded it with function-preserving operators to 51B (40B more tokens) and to 101B (27B more). The run took 21.5 days on 192 A800 GPUs, against an estimated 76.7 days for a 101B model from scratch, for about $100K ([arXiv:2309.03852](https://arxiv.org/abs/2309.03852) (Li et al., 2023, preprint)). The headline, 80% of the baselines' scores for 10% of their FLOPs, flatters the result. On four leaderboard tasks it averaged 43.9, against 54.3 for Llama 2 7B, which had about 4× the training compute of FLM's English share. Its MMLU of 28.3 is close to chance (25%), and there was no from-scratch 101B run to compare against.

The same accounting gives the carbon bill. Energy is hours × processors × measured average power × the data centre's overhead (PUE). Emissions are energy × the grid's carbon intensity. Patterson et al. put GPT-3's training at 1,287 MWh and 552 tCO2e: 10,000 V100s for 14.8 days, at PUE 1.1 and 0.429 kg CO2e per kWh. Some of those inputs are guesses: the GPU count comes from an NVIDIA press release, and 0.429 is the US average because the Azure site was not reported. **They estimate that the choice of model, data centre and processor can shrink the footprint by about 100–1000×.** They also cut an earlier, widely quoted estimate for a neural-architecture search by 88× ([arXiv:2104.10350](https://arxiv.org/abs/2104.10350) (Patterson et al., 2021, preprint)). The grid matters as much as the GPUs. In OLMo's comparison table, BLOOM-176B used 433 MWh but emitted only 30 t on France's low-carbon grid, against 380 t for Gopher's 1,066 MWh. OLMo-7B's A100 run in Australia took 104 MWh and emitted about 70 t, while its twin on LUMI's hydro-powered MI250Xs counted as zero (about 3.5 t at a typical hydro intensity of 0.024 kg/kWh) ([arXiv:2402.00838](https://arxiv.org/abs/2402.00838) (Groeneveld et al., ACL 2024)). OLMo 2 added water: 202 kL for the 7B and 892 kL for the 13B, next to 52 and 101 tCO2e. On the same table, Llama 3.1 8B used 1,022 MWh and 420 tCO2e, against 131 MWh for OLMo 2 7B ([arXiv:2501.00656](https://arxiv.org/abs/2501.00656) (OLMo et al., 2024, preprint)). These are lower bounds. They count the final run's electricity only, not hardware manufacture, failed and tuning runs, or inference, and Patterson et al. are Google authors measuring mostly Google hardware. OPT-175B shows what the final-run figure leaves out. Meta trained it on 992 80GB A100s, at up to 147 TFLOP/s per GPU, and estimated 75 tCO2e, against 500 t it attributes to GPT-3 (Patterson's table says 552) and 380 t for Gopher. Over two months, hardware failures forced at least 35 manual restarts and the cycling of more than 100 hosts. Loss divergences were handled by lowering the learning rate and rewinding to an earlier checkpoint. Counting ablations, baselines and downtime, the authors put their own total at roughly 2× the 75 t ([arXiv:2205.01068](https://arxiv.org/abs/2205.01068) (Zhang et al., 2022, preprint)). BLOOM's report counts more of the life cycle. Training the 176B model took 3.5 months on 384 A100s, and it estimates 81 tCO2e. Of that, 25 t was electricity for training (OLMo's table lists 30 t), 11 t was hardware manufacture and 45 t was the cluster's idle draw. The final run was about 37% of the project's compute emissions. At 57 gCO2e/kWh, its 433 MWh emitted about a third of OPT's 70 t (BLOOM's figure for OPT) from 324 MWh at 231 g. It had one loss spike and 1–2 GPU failures a week, absorbed by spare nodes and checkpoints every three hours. The authors credit bfloat16 for the stability: their earlier float16 runs at 104B had diverged irreversibly ([arXiv:2211.05100](https://arxiv.org/abs/2211.05100) (BigScience Workshop, 2022, preprint)). PaLM 540B used 2.56 × 10²⁴ FLOPs: 1,200 hours on 6,144 TPU v4 chips plus 336 on 3,072, including downtime. It was trained in an Oklahoma data centre (PUE 1.08) running on 89% carbon-free energy at 0.079 tCO2e/MWh, and the estimate is 271 tCO2e. The same paper introduced *model FLOPs utilization* (MFU): achieved tokens per second divided by what the chips' peak FLOP/s would allow, without counting recomputed activations. PaLM reached 46.2%. By the paper's own calculation, GPT-3 reached 21.3% and Megatron-Turing NLG 30.2% ([arXiv:2204.02311](https://arxiv.org/abs/2204.02311) (Chowdhery et al., JMLR 2023)).

### Scaling, in practice

Chinchilla (note 01, Scale) trained 400 models from 70M to over 16B parameters on 5B to 500B tokens and found the compute-optimal ratio is about 20 training tokens per parameter, scaled together: double the model, double the data (*AI Engineering*, ch. 2). Three qualifications from Huyen:

- **Compute-optimal is not deployment-optimal.** Llama was deliberately trained past the Chinchilla point: a smaller model on more tokens costs more to train but less to run. Sardana et al. (2023) rewrote the scaling law to include expected inference demand. Mistral 7B is the case for it. On Mistral's own re-run of every benchmark it beat Llama 2 13B on all of them (MMLU 60.1% against 55.6%), and on reasoning and MMLU it scored like a Llama 2 more than 3× its size. On knowledge (NaturalQuestions, TriviaQA) the factor was only 1.9×, which the authors put down to how much a 7B model can store ([arXiv:2310.06825](https://arxiv.org/abs/2310.06825) (Jiang et al., 2023, preprint)). They call scaling a three-way problem: capability, training cost and inference cost. They never disclosed the training data or token count, so the training-cost side cannot be checked. Llama 3 8B's 15T tokens is close to 2,000 tokens per parameter. Qwen2.5 trained every size on the same 18T tokens, which is about 36,000 tokens per parameter at 0.5B ([arXiv:2412.15115](https://arxiv.org/abs/2412.15115) (Qwen Team, 2024-12)). The payoff is at deployment. Quantized to 4 bits, phi-3-mini takes about 1.8GB and runs at more than 12 tokens per second on an iPhone 14 ([arXiv:2404.14219](https://arxiv.org/abs/2404.14219) (Abdin et al., 2024-04)).
- **Returns diminish.** Going from a cross-entropy of about 3.4 to 2.8 nats takes ten times more data, yet users notice the difference.
- **Hyperparameters cannot be searched at full size.** Teams tune on small models and extrapolate. Microsoft and OpenAI's μTransfer moved hyperparameters found on a 40M model to a 6.7B one.

**The 20 survived a re-check, but the formula is shakier than it looks.** Chinchilla's parametric fit had been mis-optimized. Refitted, it agrees with the other two approaches on about 20 tokens per parameter, though with an interval of 4 to 40 at 1e26 FLOPs ([arXiv:2404.10102](https://arxiv.org/abs/2404.10102) (Besiroglu et al., 2024, preprint); details in note 01, Scale). The fitted law also adds a size term to a data term, so the benefit of more data cannot depend on model size. Skaling raises that sum to a fitted power k. On Farseer's 404 runs up to 6.4B parameters, k came out at 0.41, and extrapolation error to the largest models fell from 1.48% to 0.47% MAPE. One order of magnitude past the data, at 2e25 FLOPs, the recommended token-to-parameter ratios sit more than 10× apart: about 380 for Chinchilla's fit, against 20–40 for Skaling and for a fit-free estimate read off the runs ([arXiv:2608.07222](https://arxiv.org/abs/2608.07222) (Videau et al., 2026, preprint)). Skaling fitted only to an L-shaped grid of cheap runs (small models swept over data, short runs swept over size), about a tenth of the fitting compute, still matched or beat Chinchilla fitted to the full grid. That result depends on the data. On Chinchilla's own measurements (k = 0.77) and on Farseer's code subset, k is 0.77 to 0.90 and the gain disappears. On the authors' own grid the recommended ratio moves in the opposite direction with compute, so the sign of the correction depends on data and recipe.

**"Compute-optimal" also depends on which loss you scale.** Roberts et al. ran IsoFLOP sweeps from 6e18 to 3e21 FLOPs on a Llama-3-style recipe, with models from 40M to 8B parameters, and fitted a separate optimum for each skill. Knowledge QA wanted more parameters and code wanted more tokens. The gap held after they equalized the share of each skill's data in the mix; the two optima only lined up when code made up about 2.1× as much of the mix as knowledge data. At the smallest budget, changing the datamix moved one skill's optimal parameter count by almost 50%, and just changing the validation set moved the optimal parameter count by more than 30%. At the three largest budgets it still moved it by more than 10% ([arXiv:2503.10061](https://arxiv.org/abs/2503.10061) (Roberts et al., 2025, preprint)). Mixed-modal training adds a term for interaction between modalities. Over 250 runs on seven modalities, with laws fitted on models up to 6.7B and tested at 30B, Aghajanyan et al. found modalities compete in small models and help each other past a size their law predicts. For speech and text, the predicted point was about 28B parameters on 45B tokens, and a 30B model trained on 50B tokens did cross from competition into synergy ([arXiv:2301.03728](https://arxiv.org/abs/2301.03728) (Aghajanyan et al., ICML 2023)). All of this is perplexity, mostly at one epoch.

*Building AI Agents* (ch. 3) adds the emergent-abilities debate covered in note 01 and the Kaplan law; it adds nothing to either.

### Continued pretraining

To add knowledge, run more pretraining: the same next-token loss on raw, unlabeled domain text, before any SFT. Gururangan et al. named it *domain-adaptive pretraining* (DAPT) ([arXiv:2004.10964](https://arxiv.org/abs/2004.10964) (Gururangan et al., ACL 2020)). A second pretraining phase of RoBERTa on domain text raised macro-F1 on ACL-ARC citation intent from 63.0 to 75.4, and micro-F1 on ChemProt from 81.9 to 84.2. **The gain tracks how far the domain is from the original corpus.** On AG News, whose news domain RoBERTa had already seen, the gain was zero, and adapting to an *unrelated* domain made most tasks worse. *Task-adaptive* pretraining, on just the task's own unlabeled text, cost far less and beat DAPT on five of the eight tasks. The domain text need not be hand-curated. DSIR (see What the data is) picked 25M Pile documents matching each task's unlabeled text and averaged 83.76 F1 over the same eight tasks, against 83.46 for DAPT's curated corpora and 82.58 for random Pile text ([arXiv:2302.03169](https://arxiv.org/abs/2302.03169) (Xie et al., NeurIPS 2023)).

The recipe scales to LLMs at a small fraction of the original cost:

- **CodeGen** ([arXiv:2203.13474](https://arxiv.org/abs/2203.13474) (Nijkamp et al., ICLR 2023)) chained the stages. It trained models from 350M to 16.1B parameters first on the Pile (355B natural-language tokens plus 32B of code), then on 119B tokens of code in six languages, then on 72B tokens of Python only. At 16.1B, HumanEval pass@1 went from 14.2 to 18.3 to 29.3 across the three stages, level with Codex-12B (28.8). The same paper built a multi-turn benchmark of 115 problems split into at least three prompts each. Asking in turns beat one concatenated prompt (47.3 vs 38.7 pass rate at 16.1B), but not on the easiest problems, where the gain vanished at 6.1B and above.
- **Code Llama** ([arXiv:2308.12950](https://arxiv.org/abs/2308.12950) (Rozière et al., 2023, preprint)) started from Llama 2 and trained on 500B tokens (1T for the 70B): 85% code, 8% natural language about code and 7% general text kept to limit forgetting. Starting from Llama 2 paid off. A 7B trained from scratch on the same mix ended at the loss Code Llama 7B had reached about 240B tokens earlier, and the gap was still widening. On Python, Code Llama 7B roughly matched Llama 2 70B (HumanEval 33.5 vs 30.5). A further 100B Python-heavy tokens added 4.3–8.3 points of HumanEval pass@1. Infilling training cost little: 0.6 points at 7B and 1.1 at 13B, averaged over HumanEval and MBPP pass@1/10/100. The long-context stage cost 0.52 HumanEval points and 1.9 MBPP points on short prompts (RoPE details in [note 01](01-how-llms-work.md#embeddings-and-position)). The evaluations are Meta's own.
- **ChipNeMo** ([arXiv:2311.00176](https://arxiv.org/abs/2311.00176) (Liu et al., 2023-11, preprint)) continued LLaMA 2 on 24B tokens of NVIDIA's internal chip-design documents and code, then ran SFT. For the 7B model, DAPT took 2,620 A100-hours and SFT 90, against 184,320 for the original pretraining: under 1.5% extra. ChipNeMo-70B beat GPT-4 on two of three internal tasks (an engineering-assistant chatbot and EDA script generation). DAPT through LoRA adapters left a significant accuracy gap against full-parameter DAPT. The benchmarks are NVIDIA's own and unreleased.
- **LLaMA Pro** ([arXiv:2401.02415](https://arxiv.org/abs/2401.02415) (Wu et al., ACL 2024)) attacks forgetting by adding parameters. It copies 8 transformer blocks into LLaMA2-7B, zero-initializes their output projections so the model starts unchanged, and trains only the new blocks, on 80B tokens of code and math. Against the base model, HumanEval pass@1 rose from 13.1 to 28.7 and GSM8K from 14.5 to 17.9, while MMLU held (46.9 to 47.9). In its law-domain ablation, the 8-block expansion kept general scores (56.7 average) better than full fine-tuning (55.0) and learned the domain task better than LoRA (75.1 vs 65.3 on Unfair-ToS). LoRA preserved general ability but struggled to learn the new domain.
- **Branch-Train-MiX** ([arXiv:2403.07816](https://arxiv.org/abs/2403.07816) (Sukhbaatar et al., 2024-03, preprint)) runs continued pretraining for several domains in parallel and then merges the copies into one mixture-of-experts model (see [note 01](01-how-llms-work.md#reading-a-models-size)). Starting from Llama 2 7B, it trained separate copies on math (201B tokens), code (210B) and Wikipedia (42B). Each copy's feed-forward blocks became an expert, next to the original model's, and the attention weights were averaged. A router was then trained on 80B more tokens, picking the top 2 experts per token. Averaged over math, code, knowledge, reasoning and general tasks it scored 47.9. On the same data, upcycling the seed into an MoE scored 46.3, a dense model 44.5 and Llama 2 13B 45.4. Upcycling still won narrowly on math and code, and with compute matched rather than data, the gap closes to 47.9 against 47.3. The test covers three domains and four experts, with no SFT or RLHF afterwards.
- **Qwen2.5-Coder** ([arXiv:2409.12186](https://arxiv.org/abs/2409.12186) (Hui et al., 2024, preprint)) continued every Qwen2.5 size from 0.5B to 32B on 5.5T tokens. That was 5.2T of single files at 8K context, then 300B of whole repositories at 32K with the RoPE base raised from 10,000 to 1,000,000, the same change Code Llama made. Its mix ablation at 7B tested 100% code, 85:10:5 and 70:20:10 code, text and maths. Pure code scored worst on average (31.3 against 55.0 for 70:20:10), mostly because maths and general scores collapsed (GSM8K 23.8 against 64.5). The authors say 70:20:10 also beat the code-heavier mixes on code, but their table shows pure code slightly ahead there (49.8 against 48.3 on the table's "common" code benchmarks, 40.3 against 38.3 on BigCodeBench). The quality filters for code-related web text were small fastText classifiers; larger models brought no significant gain. Four rounds of filtering raised a 1.5B model's HumanEval/MBPP average from 41.6 to 46.8.

## Supervised fine-tuning

Huyen's vocabulary (*AI Engineering*, ch. 1) is worth adopting. **Pre-training** builds the base model and is by far the expensive part: for InstructGPT it took about 98% of the compute, post-training 2%. **Post-training** is everything the model developer does afterwards (SFT, preference tuning). **Fine-tuning** is the same machinery when an application developer does it to someone else's model. Changing a prompt is not training of any kind; no weight moves.

SFT continues training on curated examples of the behaviour you want. The examples are expensive because they are written, not scraped: OpenAI used about 13,000 (prompt, response) pairs for InstructGPT, which at $10 a pair would come to $130,000 before task design and quality control. A pair can take up to 30 minutes to write, and about 90% of InstructGPT's demonstration writers had a college degree (*AI Engineering*, ch. 2). *Building AI Agents* (ch. 3) puts typical SFT sets at 10,000 to 100,000 pairs. The idea is older than chat. *Instruction tuning* began with FLAN ([arXiv:2109.01652](https://arxiv.org/abs/2109.01652) (Wei et al., ICLR 2022)) and T0 ([arXiv:2110.08207](https://arxiv.org/abs/2110.08207) (Sanh et al., ICLR 2022)), which fine-tuned on dozens of existing NLP datasets rewritten as instructions. FLAN's 137B model then beat zero-shot GPT-3 175B on 20 of 25 held-out datasets. The lineage, and what each successor learned, is in [05-RL note 02](../../05-RL/notes/02-rlhf-pipeline.md#stage-1--supervised-fine-tuning-sft). Raschka builds two flavours (*LLMs from Scratch*, ch. 6–7):

| | Classification fine-tuning (ch. 6) | Instruction fine-tuning (ch. 7) |
|---|---|---|
| Change to the model | Replace the output head with a small one (e.g. 2 classes); usually train only the last block(s) and the head | None; keep the language-model head |
| Data | Labeled examples (spam / not spam) | Instruction–response pairs in a fixed template (Alpaca-style) |
| Loss | Cross-entropy on the last token's class logits | Next-token loss on the response; padding and usually the prompt tokens are masked out |
| Evaluation | Accuracy | Hard; Raschka scores responses with a larger LLM as judge |

*Building AI Agents* (ch. 2) shows the classification route on a small decoder, with a linear head on the last token's hidden state; it is the same pattern as *LLMs from Scratch* ch. 6.

The *LLM Engineer's Handbook* (ch. 5) adds the practical side: building an instruction dataset from your own documents, deduplication, using an LLM to generate and filter pairs, chat templates, and choosing between full fine-tuning, LoRA and QLoRA by GPU budget.

**Rule:** SFT teaches the *form* of an answer far more than new facts. Fine-tuning on a knowledge base to make a model "know" it tends to produce confident paraphrases with errors. For facts that change or need citations, use retrieval ([02-rag-retrieval](../../02-rag-retrieval/README.md)); for a large, stable body of domain text, use continued pretraining (above).

The evidence for the rule:

- **Little data, mostly format.** Flan-PaLM's instruction tuning used 1.4B tokens, 0.2% of its 780B pretraining tokens, and most of its gain came from the first 282 of 1,836 tasks. The authors' reading is that the model was learning to express knowledge it already had ([arXiv:2210.11416](https://arxiv.org/abs/2210.11416) (Chung et al., JMLR 2024)). LIMA's *Superficial Alignment Hypothesis* says the same from 1,000 examples ([arXiv:2305.11206](https://arxiv.org/abs/2305.11206) (Zhou et al., NeurIPS 2023)).
- **New facts make it hallucinate.** In a controlled study on PaLM 2-S closed-book QA, examples the model didn't already know were fit far more slowly than known ones. **As they were fit, test accuracy fell linearly**, so the model got worse at using what it knew. Early stopping, or relabelling unknown answers "I don't know", avoided most of the damage ([arXiv:2405.05904](https://arxiv.org/abs/2405.05904) (Gekhman et al., EMNLP 2024)).
- **Not much semantics is needed either.** The instruction-tuning survey ([arXiv:2308.10792](https://arxiv.org/abs/2308.10792) (Zhang et al., 2023-08, preprint)) collects the critique. In Kung and Peng (2023), models tuned on task definitions stripped of meaning, or on examples with *wrong* input–output pairs, scored close to models tuned on the real ones.

Fine-tuning can also erase what pretraining taught. The effect shrinks with scale and can be offset by replaying old data. When PaLM-E unfroze its LLM for multimodal training, its language-generation score dropped by 87.3% (relative) at 12B but by only 3.9% at 562B ([arXiv:2303.03378](https://arxiv.org/abs/2303.03378) (Driess et al., 2023-03)). Qwen-VL mixed pure-text samples into its multimodal training and held MMLU at 50.7, against 49.9 for the LLM it started from ([arXiv:2308.12966](https://arxiv.org/abs/2308.12966) (Bai et al., 2023-08)).

Replay works, but it gets more expensive as a model accumulates training stages. The alternative is to limit which weights move. Two examples are LLaMA Pro's frozen-base block expansion (Continued pretraining, above) and sparse memory finetuning ([arXiv:2510.15103](https://arxiv.org/abs/2510.15103) (Lin et al., 2025-10, preprint)). Sparse memory finetuning needs a model with a *memory layer*: a pool of 1M slots, of which each token reads only 32. On each step it updates only the slots that the new data uses much more than pretraining data did (a TF-IDF ranking). **Trained on a stream of TriviaQA facts to the same recall, a 1.3B model lost 89% of its NaturalQuestions F1 under full fine-tuning, 71% under LoRA and 11% under sparse memory finetuning.** The result covers fact learning only, at 1.3B, on a memory-layer architecture you would have to pretrain yourself.

## Preference optimization, briefly

SFT can only imitate. To make a model prefer the better of two acceptable answers, you train on comparisons:

- **RLHF:** train a reward model on human rankings, then optimize the policy against it with PPO and a KL penalty to the SFT model.
  - *The data is comparisons, because they are cheaper than writing.* For Llama 2, each comparison cost Meta about $3.50, against $25 for a written response, and LMSYS found a careful comparison takes three to five minutes. Agreement is limited: InstructGPT's labelers agreed with each other about 73% of the time (*AI Engineering*, ch. 2).
  - *The reward model turns rankings into a score.* For a prompt `x` with a preferred answer `y_w` and a rejected one `y_l`, InstructGPT minimizes `−log σ(r_θ(x, y_w) − r_θ(x, y_l))`: push the winner's score above the loser's. Three ranked answers give three such pairs.
  - *A reward model is useful on its own.* Stitch Fix and Grab skip the RL step: they sample several answers and serve the one their reward model scores highest (best-of-N, note 01). OpenAI's WebGPT found the same: on long-form questions answered by browsing, keeping the best of 64 samples was preferred to the plain fine-tuned 175B model 68% of the time, against 58% for PPO on the same reward model ([arXiv:2112.09332](https://arxiv.org/abs/2112.09332) (Nakano et al., 2021, preprint)).
- **DPO** ([arXiv:2305.18290](https://arxiv.org/abs/2305.18290)): skip the reward model; optimize directly on (prompt, chosen, rejected) triples. The reward model's optimal policy has a closed form, so RLHF reduces to a classification loss with no sampling during training. It beat PPO-based RLHF at controlling sentiment and matched or beat it on summarization and single-turn dialogue. Meta used RLHF for Llama 2 and switched to DPO for Llama 3 (*AI Engineering*, ch. 2). *LLM Engineer's Handbook* ch. 6 walks through it.
- **Constitutional AI / RLAIF** ([arXiv:2212.08073](https://arxiv.org/abs/2212.08073)): a model critiques and ranks outputs against written principles in place of human labelers.

All three are covered in [05-RL notes 02–04](../../05-RL/README.md#syllabus).

## Parameter-efficient fine-tuning

Full fine-tuning updates every weight and needs optimizer state for all of them, several times the model's memory. PEFT methods train a small number of new parameters and freeze the rest ([arXiv:2312.12148](https://arxiv.org/abs/2312.12148), Xu et al., 2023, survey them). *Building AI Agents* (ch. 3) walks through the older options. Pfeiffer et al.'s survey ([arXiv:2302.11529](https://arxiv.org/abs/2302.11529) (Pfeiffer et al., TMLR 2023)) sorts them by where the new parameters go: into the existing weights (BitFit, LoRA), into the input (prompt and prefix tuning), or as new layers (adapters). All of them reduce to "frozen output plus a learned correction". Their reading of the comparisons: prompt tuning loses from limited capacity, bias-only tuning has the smallest footprint but lower scores, and adapters score best but add parameters.

- **Adapters** ([arXiv:1902.00751](https://arxiv.org/abs/1902.00751) (Houlsby et al., ICML 2019)) insert small bottleneck layers inside each block: for BERT, project 1,024 dimensions down to 24 and back up, under 50K parameters per adapter. Training only these (3.6% of the parameters) came close to full fine-tuning on GLUE. Unlike merged LoRA, adapters add a little latency at inference.
- **Prefix tuning** ([doi:10.18653/v1/2021.acl-long.353](https://doi.org/10.18653/v1/2021.acl-long.353) (Li & Liang, ACL 2021)) learns key/value vectors prepended at every layer: 250K parameters (0.1%) on GPT-2 Medium. It matched full fine-tuning on table-to-text and beat it by 2.9 BLEU on average with 50–500 training examples, an edge that shrank as the data grew. On XSUM summarization it trailed, 35.05 vs 37.25 ROUGE-L at 0.1%. Optimising the prefix directly was unstable, so the authors had to reparametrise it through an MLP during training.
- **Prompt tuning** ([arXiv:2104.08691](https://arxiv.org/abs/2104.08691) (Lester et al., EMNLP 2021)) keeps only the input-layer soft tokens: 20,480 parameters per task for a 5-token prompt on T5-XXL. **It works only at scale.** On SuperGLUE it lags model tuning badly at T5-Small through Large, and matches even multi-task model tuning only at 11B. The smaller sizes also depended on first continuing T5's training with a plain language-modelling objective; the 11B model worked without it. Its upside is robustness: trained on SQuAD, it beat model tuning on most out-of-domain QA sets, by 12.5 F1 on TextbookQA.
- **BitFit** ([doi:10.18653/v1/2022.acl-short.1](https://doi.org/10.18653/v1/2022.acl-short.1) (Ben Zaken et al., ACL 2022)) trains only the bias terms, 0.08% of BERT-Large, and matched full fine-tuning on the GLUE dev average (84.2 vs 84.1). The win is small-data only. It fell behind on the largest task, QQP (85.4 vs 87.5), and on SQuAD the ordering flipped once more training data was available. LLaMA-family models have no bias terms in their linear layers, so it does not carry over to them as is.
- **LLaMA-Adapter** ([arXiv:2303.16199](https://arxiv.org/abs/2303.16199) (Zhang et al., ICLR 2024)) brought prefix-style prompts to instruction tuning. It inserts learned prompts into the top 30 layers of a frozen LLaMA-7B, with a gate initialised at zero so they start out with no effect: 1.2M parameters and one hour on 8 A100s, against Alpaca's three hours of full fine-tuning on the same 52K examples. The zero gate is the trick. With random initialisation, ScienceQA validation accuracy was 40.77%, near chance; with the zero gate it was 83.85%. Its quality claim against Alpaca rests on GPT-4 judging 80 Vicuna questions, which is thin evidence.

**Where they lose to LoRA.** Hu et al. ran the old methods side by side on GPT-3 175B. On WikiSQL, full fine-tuning scored 73.8, BitFit 71.3, input-layer prompt tuning 63.1, per-layer prefixes 70.1, adapters 71.9–73.2 and LoRA 73.4–74.0 (Table 4). WikiSQL pairs 80,654 crowd-paraphrased questions with SQL queries over 24,241 Wikipedia tables. Each table sits in only one split, so a model must handle schemas it has not seen ([arXiv:1709.00103](https://arxiv.org/abs/1709.00103) (Zhong et al., 2017, preprint)). Its authors scored it two ways because each metric fails differently. Execution accuracy credits a wrong query that happens to return the right rows, and exact match on the query text penalizes a correct query whose conditions are in a different order. Their own Seq2SQL, an LSTM pointer network trained in part with execution results as an RL reward, reached 59.4% execution accuracy and 48.3% exact match on the test set. Only LoRA matched or beat full fine-tuning on that task. With few examples the ranking changes. (IA)³ learns three vectors per layer that rescale the attention keys, values and feed-forward activations, 540K parameters on T0-3B. On nine held-out tasks with 20–70 training examples each, it was the only PEFT method tested, LoRA (9.1M parameters) included, to beat full fine-tuning. The full recipe, T-Few, on T0 11B scored 72.4% against 66.6% for few-shot in-context learning with GPT-3 175B, at about 1,000× fewer inference FLOPs per example. Fine-tuning cost about as much as GPT-3 spends on 20 in-context examples, half an hour on one A100 ([arXiv:2205.05638](https://arxiv.org/abs/2205.05638) (Liu et al., NeurIPS 2022)). Every task was classification or multiple choice, scored by ranking the answer options, so this says nothing about generation.

**LoRA** ([arXiv:2106.09685](https://arxiv.org/abs/2106.09685), Hu et al., 2021) is the default. For a frozen weight matrix `W` (d × k), learn a low-rank update:

```
W' = W + ΔW,   ΔW = B · A,   B: d × r,  A: r × k,  r ≪ min(d, k)
```

- Only `A` and `B` are trained. On GPT-3 175B the paper reports 10,000× fewer trainable parameters and 3× less GPU memory than full fine-tuning, with comparable quality.
- The update can be merged into `W` after training, so inference costs nothing extra; or kept separate so one base model serves many adapters.
- What LoRA is *not*: *Prompt Engineering for GenAI* (ch. 2) calls it "low-rank approximations" that make the network run on consumer hardware. LoRA is low-rank *adaptation*. It shrinks the number of *trainable* parameters during fine-tuning; it does not make the model smaller or faster at inference. Shrinking the model is quantization, pruning or distillation (below).
- **QLoRA** ([arXiv:2305.14314](https://arxiv.org/abs/2305.14314)) keeps the frozen base in 4-bit and trains LoRA adapters on top, which brings 65B-parameter fine-tuning onto a single 48 GB GPU. Three tricks make it fit: a 4-bit NormalFloat data type, quantizing the quantization constants themselves (about 3 GB saved at 65B), and paged optimizers that absorb memory spikes. Its Guanaco models reached 99.3% of ChatGPT's score on the Vicuna benchmark after 24 hours on one GPU. The authors themselves warn that those chatbot benchmarks are not trustworthy, so read the number as "close", not "equal".
- **GaLore** ([arXiv:2403.03507](https://arxiv.org/abs/2403.03507) (Zhao et al., 2024-03)) targets optimizer memory instead of the trainable-parameter count. It projects each gradient into a low-rank subspace, cutting optimizer-state memory by up to 65.5%. That is enough to pre-train LLaMA-7B on a 24 GB consumer GPU. Unlike LoRA it updates all the weights, so it also works for pre-training. Its 8-bit 7B run on C4 reached a validation perplexity of 14.65 after 150K steps, a single run.
- **LoRA+** ([arXiv:2402.12354](https://arxiv.org/abs/2402.12354) (Hayou et al., ICML 2024)) gives `B` a larger learning rate than `A`. The authors' width-scaling argument says a shared rate under-trains `B`. It is the same compute as LoRA, with 1–2% better scores and up to 2× faster convergence, mostly on hard tasks. On Llama-7B trained on a 100K flan-v2 subset, MMLU rose about 1.3 points; on MNLI, which Llama finds easy, equal rates were already near-optimal. The best ratio varies by model and task: about 2⁴ for RoBERTa, 2¹–2² for Llama.
- **LongLoRA** ([arXiv:2309.12307](https://arxiv.org/abs/2309.12307) (Chen et al., ICLR 2024)) found the case where plain LoRA fails: **extending the context window**. Taking Llama 2 7B to 32K tokens, LoRA reached a PG19 perplexity of 11.44 at rank 8, against 8.08 for full fine-tuning, and rank 256 did no better (11.98). Making the embedding and normalisation layers trainable as well (about 2% of the parameters) brought it to 8.12. With shifted sparse attention during training it took Llama 2 7B to 100K tokens, or 70B to 32K, on one 8× A100 machine.
- **LOMO** ([arXiv:2306.09782](https://arxiv.org/abs/2306.09782) (Lv et al., ACL 2024)) keeps *full* fine-tuning and removes the optimizer state instead. It uses plain SGD and applies each layer's update as soon as its gradient is computed, so the full gradient is never held in memory. For LLaMA-7B (batch 8, length 512, activation checkpointing) that is 14.6 GB against 102.2 GB for AdamW. It also trained a 65B model on 8× RTX 3090s. On six SuperGLUE tasks with 1,000 examples it beat LoRA by 2.8 points on average at 13B. That is a narrow test, and it is SGD, not Adam.
- **MeZO** ([arXiv:2305.17333](https://arxiv.org/abs/2305.17333) (Malladi et al., NeurIPS 2023)) drops backpropagation altogether. It estimates the gradient from two forward passes with a shared random perturbation, so training needs only inference memory. One A100 80GB can then fine-tune a 30B model, where backprop with Adam fits 2.7B. On OPT-13B with 1,000 examples it came within 1% of full fine-tuning on 7 of 11 tasks, using 12× less memory. The gaps elsewhere were large: 67.9 vs 83.9 on CB, 60.1 vs 71.1 on MultiRC. It needs many more steps (20K vs 625) and works only when the task is phrased as a prompt.

**Without the weights.** Three methods adapt a model whose weights you cannot touch, and each needs something closed APIs do not give.
- Black-box tuning ([arXiv:2201.03514](https://arxiv.org/abs/2201.03514) (Sun et al., ICML 2022)) searches a 500-dimensional random projection of a soft prompt with CMA-ES, using 8,000 API calls. On RoBERTa-Large with 16 examples per class it averaged 73.0 across seven tasks, against 63.5 for gradient prompt tuning and 78.9 for full model tuning. It needs an API that accepts prompt embeddings and returns logits.
- Offsite-tuning ([arXiv:2302.04870](https://arxiv.org/abs/2302.04870) (Xiao et al., 2023-02, preprint)) has the model owner send a few top and bottom layers plus a compressed "emulator" of the middle. The data owner tunes those layers and sends them back. On GPT-2 XL and OPT-1.3B the plugged-back model came within a few points of full fine-tuning on most tasks. Combined with LoRA it trained GPT-2 XL 6.5× faster in 5.6× less memory than full fine-tuning. The authors say they have not shown that the emulator and adapter don't leak the model or the data.
- Proxy-tuning ([arXiv:2401.08565](https://arxiv.org/abs/2401.08565) (Liu et al., COLM 2024)) works at decoding time. It adds the logit difference between a small tuned model and its untuned base to a large base model's logits. Llama-2-7B-Chat minus Llama-2-7B, applied to Llama-2-70B, closed 88% of the gap to Llama-2-70B-Chat across the paper's benchmarks, and it beat the chat model on open-ended TruthfulQA (85.1 vs 79.6% informative and true), though not on the multiple-choice version (59.2 vs 68.3). Reasoning did not transfer: GSM8K was 32.0 against 51.8 for the real chat model. It needs a shared vocabulary and full logits (top-5 for their GPT-3.5 case), and it ran about 1.5× slower at 70B.

**Adapters as reusable parts.** With one base model and many LoRAs, new adapters can be assembled from old ones without training.
- LoraHub ([arXiv:2307.13269](https://arxiv.org/abs/2307.13269) (Huang et al., COLM 2024)) picks weights for a mix of existing LoRAs, with gradient-free search on five examples. On Big-Bench Hard with FLAN-T5-Large it scored 34.7, below 5-shot in-context learning (37.3), LoRA (37.7) and full fine-tuning (42.1). It used a fifth of in-context learning's tokens per example.
- Text-to-LoRA ([arXiv:2506.06105](https://arxiv.org/abs/2506.06105) (Charakorn et al., ICML 2025)) trains a hypernetwork on 479 tasks to write a LoRA from a task description in one forward pass. On Mistral-7B its adapters for unseen tasks averaged 73.9 on eight benchmarks, against 71.9 for a multi-task LoRA and 75.8 for LoRAs trained on each task. Its descriptions were written by GPT-4o-mini, and the authors expect worse results from what users actually type.

```python
from peft import LoraConfig, get_peft_model
config = LoraConfig(r=16, lora_alpha=32, target_modules=["q_proj", "v_proj"], lora_dropout=0.05)
model = get_peft_model(base_model, config)   # model.print_trainable_parameters() -> well under 1%
```

### Making the model smaller

Three ways to cut memory and latency once a model is trained (*Building AI Agents*, ch. 2–3):

- **Quantization** stores weights in fewer bits. The basic affine scheme maps a float `x` to an integer `x_q = round(x / s) + z`, with a scale `s` and zero point `z` chosen per tensor, channel or block. Halving the bits halves the weight memory (note 01, Reading a model's size). Fewer bits do not always mean faster, though. Lu et al. ran Phi-1.5 under llama.cpp on a Jetson Orin NX GPU with prompts of up to 50 tokens. 4-bit k-quants (Q4_K_M) cut prefill latency by about 50% on average, and the formats tested cut decode latency by 17–75%. The 3-bit and 6-bit formats were about as slow as FP16 at prefill by 50 tokens, because hardware has no fast path for irregular bit widths ([arXiv:2409.15790](https://arxiv.org/abs/2409.15790) (Lu et al., 2024, preprint)). Decode gains more because it is limited by memory bandwidth, not compute.
- **Pruning** removes weights or whole layers. Classic prune-and-retrain cycles are too expensive at LLM scale, and the book reports that many one-shot methods fail to remove more than about 10% of weights before the model collapses. SparseGPT reached 60% sparsity on the 175B-class models OPT-175B and BLOOM-176B without retraining (the book says "170-billion-parameter models"). Wanda also needs no retraining. It holds LLaMA-7B at 7.26 perplexity at 50% unstructured sparsity, against 5.68 dense, but perplexity reaches 84.5 at 70% ([arXiv:2306.11695](https://arxiv.org/abs/2306.11695) (Sun et al., 2023-06)). Unstructured sparsity also gives no speedup on its own; see [inference 08](../../../ai-engineering/07-inference-engineering/notes/08-adaptive-inference.md#quantization). Gromov et al. dropped blocks of the deeper layers from open models and "healed" each pruned model with a small QLoRA fine-tune. **Llama-2-70B kept its 5-shot MMLU score until roughly half its layers were gone**, and across the Llama-2, Qwen, Mistral and Phi-2 models the collapse to chance came between 20% and 55% pruned. Larger, deeper models tolerated more pruning. "Little loss" holds only for knowledge question-answering, though. Next-token loss on C4 rose steadily with every layer removed, and GSM8K and HellaSwag dropped from the first layer cut. The authors read this as shallow layers storing knowledge and deeper layers doing reasoning ([arXiv:2403.17887](https://arxiv.org/abs/2403.17887) (Gromov et al., ICLR 2025)). Structured pruning pays off once distillation does the retraining. NVIDIA's Minitron recipe width-pruned Mistral NeMo 12B to 8B and retrained it on a KL loss against the teacher's logits. That took 380B tokens, against 15T to train Llama 3.1 8B from scratch, and the result scored 69.5 on 5-shot MMLU against Llama 3.1 8B's 65.3 ([arXiv:2408.11796](https://arxiv.org/abs/2408.11796) (Sreenivas et al., 2024-08, preprint)). Two practical findings. When you don't have the teacher's pretraining data, fine-tune the teacher on your distillation corpus first (about 100B tokens; they call it "teacher correction"), or its guidance is off-distribution. And at 4B from Llama 3.1 8B, width pruning beat depth pruning on accuracy, while depth pruning gave the larger speedup (2.7× vs 1.8×).
- **Distillation** trains a small *student* to match a large *teacher*'s output distribution. The recipe is Hinton, Vinyals and Dean's ([arXiv:1503.02531](https://arxiv.org/abs/1503.02531) (Hinton et al., 2015, NIPS 2014 Deep Learning Workshop)). Raise the softmax temperature T so that the teacher's *soft targets* show how it ranks the wrong answers. Train the student on those at the same T, add a lower-weighted cross-entropy on the true labels at T = 1, and multiply the soft-target gradients by T² so the mix stays balanced when T changes. Matching logits is the high-temperature limit of this loss. Label smoothing is the version without a teacher. Szegedy et al. trained Inception-v3 on targets that put 1 − ε on the true class and spread ε evenly over all K classes. That equals the usual loss plus a cross-entropy against the uniform distribution, and it keeps the top logit from growing without limit. On ImageNet (K = 1,000, ε = 0.1) it cut top-1 error from 23.1% to 22.8% ([arXiv:1512.00567](https://arxiv.org/abs/1512.00567) (Szegedy et al., CVPR 2016)). The original transformer trained with the same ε = 0.1. **Soft targets carry far more information per example than labels.** On an 85M-parameter speech model trained on only 3% of the data, hard labels overfit at 44.5% test frame accuracy, while soft targets reached 57.0%, against 58.9% for the full-data baseline. A single distilled model also kept more than 80% of a 10-model ensemble's accuracy gain. DistilBERT applied the recipe during pretraining rather than for one task ([arXiv:1910.01108](https://arxiv.org/abs/1910.01108) (Sanh et al., 2019, preprint)). The student keeps BERT-base's width, drops every other layer and starts from the teacher's remaining layers. It trains on soft targets plus the masked-LM loss and a cosine loss on hidden states. At 66M parameters against 110M, it scored 77.0 on GLUE dev against 79.5 (the "97% retained"), ran 60% faster on CPU at batch size 1, and trained in about 90 hours on 8 V100s. The average hides the spread: RTE fell from 69.3 to 59.9 and MNLI from 86.7 to 82.2. Removing the two distillation losses cost 2.96 GLUE points, and starting from random weights instead of the teacher's cost 3.69. DistilGPT-2 is a distilled GPT-2. In the LLM era the cheaper variant is to fine-tune a small model on a large model's generated outputs, which is distillation without access to the teacher's logits. Distilling the teacher's *rationales* as well as its answers cuts the data the student needs. A 770M T5 trained to output PaLM-540B's chain-of-thought rationales as a second task beat few-shot PaLM-540B on ANLI using 80% of the labeled data, which standard fine-tuning of the same T5 could not match with all of it ([arXiv:2305.02301](https://arxiv.org/abs/2305.02301) (Hsieh et al., ACL Findings 2023)). The rationale is a training-only task, so at inference the student emits just the label. The teacher also needn't be bigger, or single. FuseLLM continually trained Llama-2 7B on the token distributions of three 7B models with different architectures (Llama-2, MPT, OpenLLaMA) and gained 5.16% (relative) on BBH. Distilling from Llama-2 13B gained 2.97%, and plain continued training on the same corpus gained 1.86% ([arXiv:2401.10491](https://arxiv.org/abs/2401.10491) (Wan et al., ICLR 2024)). Gains this small are worth trying only if the models are already on hand. Google's on-device Gemini Nano models (1.8B and 3.25B) were distilled from larger Gemini models and shipped with 4-bit weights ([arXiv:2312.11805](https://arxiv.org/abs/2312.11805) (Gemini Team, 2023-12)).

Wang et al.'s survey of small language models uses the same three routes from a large model to a small one: pruning, distillation and quantization. It splits distillation by what the student can see. *White-box* distillation trains on the teacher's output distributions or internal states, as Hinton's recipe and Minitron do. *Black-box* distillation has only the teacher's generated text, as with rationale distillation ([arXiv:2411.03350](https://arxiv.org/abs/2411.03350) (Wang et al., ACM TIST 2025)). Closed APIs allow only black-box distillation. Chen & Varoquaux's survey adds when a small model is the right end point and not just a cheaper copy. They list three settings: limited compute (on-device or low-latency serving), narrow tasks with little data, and work where the decision must be explained ([arXiv:2409.06857](https://arxiv.org/abs/2409.06857) (Chen & Varoquaux, Computational Linguistics 2026)). Their MTEB plots of embedding models from about 100M parameters up to 7B show diminishing returns from size on text similarity and classification, which is one reason small encoders still do most retrieval.

Quantization for *serving* (int8/int4 weights, KV-cache compression) is an inference topic; see *LLM Engineer's Handbook* ch. 8 and [ai-engineering/07-inference-engineering](../../../ai-engineering/07-inference-engineering/README.md).

## Prompt, retrieve or fine-tune?

| Symptom | First fix | Fine-tune when |
|---|---|---|
| Wrong format or tone | Better instructions and examples ([note 03](03-prompting-fundamentals.md)) | The format is still unstable after good prompting, or the prompt is too long to pay for on every call |
| Missing or stale facts | Retrieval | Almost never; fine-tuning is a poor fact store |
| Task needs a skill the model lacks | Decompose the task; use a stronger model | You have thousands of good examples. Phoenix & Taylor's rule of thumb (*Prompt Engineering for GenAI*, ch. 1) is that fine-tuning starts to beat prompting somewhere in the low thousands of examples |
| Cost or latency | Smaller model plus a good prompt | Distil a large model's outputs into a small one |

The "skill the model lacks" row matters most for narrow classification with plenty of labels. HELM's 2022 runs found that on CivilComments toxicity detection even the best prompted LLM scored barely above chance. Yang et al.'s practitioner survey uses that result to argue that a fine-tuned small model (their cut-off is under 20B parameters) still wins such tasks. Their example is Google's Perspective API, a fine-tuned multilingual BERT with distilled CNNs, which remained among the best toxicity detectors ([arXiv:2304.13712](https://arxiv.org/abs/2304.13712) (Yang et al., TKDD 2024)).

The *LLM Engineer's Handbook*'s framing (ch. 1) is useful for whole systems: separate **feature**, **training** and **inference** pipelines (the FTI architecture), so data preparation, fine-tuning runs and serving can change independently. Its running example, the "LLM Twin", fine-tunes a model on one person's writing and serves it behind RAG.
